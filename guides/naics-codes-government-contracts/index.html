Federal Contracting Guide

NAICS codes can narrow the federal market. They do not decide contract fit by themselves.

Use NAICS as one structured signal in federal opportunity discovery, then confirm the scope, size standard, set-aside, qualifications, and performance requirements in the actual procurement.

NAICS is an industry classification system. Federal contracting offices assign a NAICS code to a procurement, and contractors can use codes to organize their capabilities and search opportunities. A code match is useful evidence of market relevance, but it is not a substitute for reading the solicitation.

What NAICS means in federal contracting

The North American Industry Classification System groups business activity into industry codes. The U.S. Census Bureau maintains the NAICS system, while federal procurement systems use NAICS codes as part of opportunity and award data.

SAM.gov supports opportunity discovery by NAICS. SBA also connects industry codes with small-business size standards, so the code assigned to a procurement can affect whether a business qualifies as small for that specific contracting context.

A business can have more than one relevant code

Many companies provide multiple services or products. Their federal market profile may therefore include a primary NAICS code and additional codes that accurately describe other lines of business. The objective is not to collect as many codes as possible; it is to represent what the business can genuinely perform.

When matching opportunities, each code should be connected to real capability: staff, equipment, licenses, delivery experience, geographic reach, and the ability to perform the work described.

Why a NAICS match is not enough

Scope can still differ

A broad industry code can contain many distinct services. Two procurements with the same NAICS code may require very different technical work.

Size standards vary

SBA size standards are tied to industry. Contractors should verify the current standard and their status for the procurement rather than relying on a generic “small business” label.

Set-asides add conditions

A procurement may be full and open, small-business set-aside, or restricted to an eligible socioeconomic program. The notice controls.

Performance factors matter

Place of performance, schedule, mandatory credentials, clearances, insurance, bonding, staffing, and past-performance expectations may determine practical fit.

Use NAICS as part of a matching stack

A stronger matching process combines NAICS with capability descriptions, certifications, geographic and staffing constraints, timing, prior work, and solicitation-specific requirements. The Registered Federal Contractors Portal uses business-profile signals to help prioritize federal opportunities for closer review.

If a match looks promising, the next question is not “Does the code match?” It is “Can this business satisfy the procurement's mandatory requirements and compete responsibly?” That is where deeper fit analysis begins.
