Federal Contracting Guide

How to find federal government contracts for your business.

Use official federal notices as the source of truth, then narrow the market with a capability profile, NAICS codes, set-aside conditions, timing, and solicitation-specific fit.

The shortest useful answer: search official federal contract opportunities on SAM.gov, but do not stop at the search result. Build a clear picture of what your business can deliver and use that profile to decide which opportunities deserve full solicitation review.

1. Start with official federal opportunity notices

SAM.gov Contract Opportunities is the federal government's centralized source for procurement notices. Anyone can search opportunities; an account adds features such as saved searches and followed notices. For a business planning to bid directly as a prime awardee, active entity registration is a separate requirement that should be verified on SAM.gov.

Search results are discovery tools. The notice, attachments, amendments, instructions, clauses, and issuing agency communications determine what the procurement actually requires.

2. Define your capability profile before you filter

A contractor should be able to describe its core services or products, geographic reach, staffing capacity, licenses, certifications, past performance, delivery model, and operational limits. This becomes the reference point for every opportunity you evaluate.

Without a capability profile, businesses often chase notices that contain familiar words but require a different scope, scale, location, credential, or delivery model.

3. Use NAICS as a market filter

Federal buyers assign NAICS codes to procurements. Those codes help contractors identify industries and search relevant opportunities. A business can be associated with more than one NAICS code when it provides multiple products or services.

However, a shared NAICS code is not proof of fit. Read the statement of work, performance requirements, evaluation factors, place of performance, and response instructions before treating an opportunity as viable.

4. Check small-business and set-aside conditions

Some federal opportunities are set aside for small businesses or for businesses participating in specific SBA contracting programs. Eligibility depends on current program rules, size standards, certification or representation requirements, and the specific procurement.

Use the opportunity notice and current SBA certification guidance to verify applicable status. Do not assume that a marketing label, profile field, or historical certification is sufficient for a current solicitation.

5. Review the opportunity beyond the headline

Scope

Can your business perform the actual work, not just a related service category?

Mandatory conditions

Identify licenses, clearances, certifications, bonding, insurance, experience, staffing, and other requirements that may be mandatory.

Schedule

Check question deadlines, site visits, response due dates, performance start dates, and delivery milestones.

Evaluation

Understand how the agency says it will evaluate offers and what evidence the proposal must provide.

6. Make a bid/no-bid decision before committing proposal resources

A good opportunity is one your business can pursue responsibly. Before investing in a response, compare the procurement with your capabilities, mandatory requirements, competitive position, staffing, financial capacity, pricing strategy, proposal workload, and performance risk.

The Registered Federal Contractors Portal supports this process with profile-based opportunity matching and additional Analyze Fit decision support. Matching and analysis can help focus review, but neither changes the official solicitation requirements or guarantees an award.
